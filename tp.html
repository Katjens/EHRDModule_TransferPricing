What Is Transfer Pricing?

Transfer pricing is the price that one part of a company charges another part of the same company for goods, services, or intangible assets such as patents and trademarks. It most often applies to multinational groups, where a parent company and its subsidiaries in different countries trade with each other.

Because these companies are related, tax authorities require that the prices match what unrelated businesses would agree to in a similar deal. This is known as the arm's length principle.

Arm's length principle: the standard that transactions between related companies should be priced as if the two parties were independent and negotiating in their own best interests. Tax authorities use it to check that profits are not shifted to low-tax countries through artificial prices.

Dividing vs. Comparing

There are two broad ways to check whether a transfer price is fair: comparing and dividing.

Comparing

Look at similar deals between unrelated companies and see whether the related-party price matches. For example, if independent companies sell the same product for $10, the related-party price should be close to $10.

Dividing

Take the total profit that the related companies earn together and split it between them based on what each one contributes, such as work done, assets used, and risks taken. This is used when the businesses are so closely linked that no good comparison exists.

Quick tip: comparing asks, "What would strangers have paid?" Dividing asks, "How should we share the profit we made together?"

Case Study: Coca-Cola

One of the largest transfer pricing disputes in history shows both approaches in action. It is a real case: The Coca-Cola Company v. Commissioner.

The Setup

Coca-Cola makes concentrate, the secret ingredient base for its drinks, and sells it through foreign affiliates called "supply points" in countries such as Ireland, Brazil, and Egypt. Those affiliates make the concentrate, and the U.S. parent owns the brand and formulas. The question: how much profit should each supply point keep, and how much should go back to the U.S. parent as royalties?

The Dividing Approach: "10-50-50"

Under a 1996 agreement with the IRS, Coca-Cola used a fixed formula known as the 10-50-50 method. In simple terms, each supply point first earned a modest set return on its sales, and the remaining profit was then split roughly 50/50 between the supply point and the U.S. parent. This divides the combined profit by formula rather than looking at what outsiders pay.

The Comparing Approach: Comparable Profits

For tax years 2007 to 2009, the IRS dropped the formula. It instead used the comparable profits method, which looks at the profits earned by independent bottling companies doing similar work, and treated those as the fair return for the supply points. Everything above that went to the U.S. parent.

Why it matters: the two approaches gave very different answers. The IRS's approach shifted billions of dollars of profit back to the U.S., where it was taxed.

What Happened

The U.S. Tax Court sided with the IRS, and Coca-Cola appealed to the Eleventh Circuit. The appeals court heard arguments in June 2026. Check for the latest status, because a ruling could change what this case teaches about which approach courts prefer.

Think about it: Coca-Cola argued it had relied on the formula for years with the IRS's blessing. The IRS argued it was never bound to accept one fixed formula forever. Which argument seems stronger to you, and why?

Case Study: Orange Business Norway

This real case went the other way from Coca-Cola. Here, a dividing approach was upheld over a comparing approach. It is Norway v. Orange Business Norway AS, decided by the Borgarting Court of Appeal in January 2020.

The Setup

Orange is a French telecommunications group. Its business division, Orange Business Services, sells communication and IT services to large corporate customers around the world. The group ran a centralized, highly integrated business model, and its Norwegian subsidiary was one piece of that global operation. The Norwegian tax authorities audited the subsidiary for the years 2006 to 2008.

The Dividing Approach: Profit Split

Orange Business Norway set its pricing using a profit split method. The group's combined profit from its global services was divided among the companies involved, based on what each one contributed. The reasoning was that the work was so intertwined across countries that no single entity could be priced on its own.

The Comparing Approach: Transactional Net Margin Method

The Norwegian tax authorities rejected this. They argued the profit split worked too much like a global formula for dividing profit, and they recalculated the subsidiary's income using the transactional net margin method, which compares its profit margin to those of nine supposedly similar independent companies.

Why it matters: the question was not just which answer was more favorable, but which method best fit the facts. When a business is deeply integrated, finding true "strangers" to compare against can be nearly impossible.

What Happened

The court ruled in favor of Orange Business Norway. It found that the company had not applied the profit split incorrectly, and that its internal pricing was backed up by a detailed analysis of the functions each group company performed. It gave little weight to the tax authorities' comparables, because those companies differed too much in the nature of their business and in size.

Think about it: compare this case to Coca-Cola. In which case was the dividing approach more convincing, and what facts made the difference?

The Arm's Length Principle

Every method you have learned so far, comparing and dividing, exists to answer one question: did the related companies price their deal the way independent companies would have? That standard is the arm's length principle, and it is the foundation of transfer pricing law worldwide.

Where It Comes From

The principle is built into most countries' tax laws and into the OECD guidelines that many countries follow. In the United States it is found in Section 482 of the Internal Revenue Code. The core idea is simple: tax authorities look at what each company actually did, then treat the related companies as if they were separate, independent businesses.

A Simple Example

A U.S. parent sells a product to its subsidiary in a low-tax country for $6. Independent buyers pay $10 for the same product. By charging the subsidiary less, the parent shifts $4 of profit per unit into the low-tax country. A tax authority would adjust the price back toward $10, which moves that profit back to where it was earned.

A Range, Not a Single Number

There is often more than one defensible arm's length price. Tax authorities usually accept a result that falls within a range of prices that independent companies actually charged. A price outside the range can be adjusted.

Connecting it back: comparing methods test whether a price matches what independent companies charge. Dividing methods test whether the profit was shared the way independent partners would have shared it. Both are ways of proving a price is at arm's length.

Arm's Length: A Simple Example

Let's put numbers on it. A U.S. company, ParentCo, makes widgets at a cost of $6 each. It sells them to a third party (an unrelated distributor) for $10 each. That $10 is the arm's length price, because it is what an independent buyer actually pays.

ParentCo also sells 1,000 widgets to its own subsidiary, SubCo, in a low-tax country. SubCo resells them to customers for $12 each. Assume the U.S. tax rate is 25% and SubCo's country taxes at 10%.

Scenario 1: ParentCo charges SubCo only $7

Profit per widget$7 - $6 = $1$12 - $7 = $5
Profit on 1,000 widgets$1,000$5,000

Total tax$750

Scenario 2: ParentCo charges the arm's length price of $10

Profit on 1,000 widgets$4,000$2,000

What This Shows

The group earns $6,000 in total profit either way. But at $7, ParentCo reports only $1,000 of it in the U.S., so $3,000 of profit shifts to the low-tax country, and the group pays $450 less tax ($1,200 minus $750). A tax authority would use the third-party price of $10 to adjust ParentCo's income back to $4,000.

Method name: using the price an unrelated buyer pays for the same product is called the comparable uncontrolled price (CUP) method. It is the most direct comparing method when a good match exists.

Comparability and Reliability

A comparing method is only as good as its comparables. Reliability describes how much you can trust the result, and it depends on how comparable the independent deals really are to the related-party deal.

What Is Intellectual Property?

Intellectual property (IP) refers to valuable creations of the mind that a company can own and legally protect. In transfer pricing, IP is often the hardest thing to price because it has no physical form. The main types are:

  • Patents: legal rights to an invention, such as a new drug or technology.
  • Trademarks: names, logos, and brands that identify a company's products.
  • Copyrights: rights to creative works, including software code, music, and writing.
  • Trade secrets and know-how: confidential information, such as a secret formula or a special process.

Ranking IP: Easiest to Hardest to Compare

The more unique an asset is, the harder it is to find independent deals to compare it with. As a rule of thumb, from easiest to hardest:

  1. Standard software and content (copyrights): licensed to many unrelated customers, so prices are easy to observe.
  2. Ordinary trademarks: everyday brands often have licensing deals and royalty rates to reference.
  3. Common patents: some similar licenses exist, but each invention differs.
  4. Trade secrets and know-how: rarely licensed in the open, so little data is available.
  5. Unique and valuable IP: breakthrough patents, world-famous brands, and secret formulas. True comparables rarely exist, so dividing methods are often used.
Link to the case studies: Coca-Cola's secret concentrate and brand are close to the top of this list, which helps explain why the dispute was so hard to settle. Orange's deeply integrated services business had a similar problem finding reliable comparables.

Ranking Circumstances: Most to Least Reliable

Comparability also depends on the circumstances around the deal, such as the market, the timing, and the economic conditions. The closer the circumstances are, the more reliable the comparison. From most to least reliable:

  1. Same product, same market, same time: the strongest comparison.
  2. Similar market, same time: small differences, easy to adjust for.
  3. Same market, different time: prices may have moved with the economy.
  4. Different market: different country, customers, competition, or market size.
  5. Very different conditions: a boom versus a recession, or very different regulation. This needs the largest adjustments and gives the least reliable result.
Note: these rankings are a teaching rule of thumb, not a legal standard. In practice, each case is judged on its own facts, and differences can often be adjusted for.

Transfer Pricing Penalties

If a tax authority decides a price was not at arm's length, it adjusts the income and the company owes more tax. On top of that, penalties can apply. The example below uses the United States, but most countries have their own penalty rules.

Two Types of Penalties

Net adjustment penalty: applies when the total transfer pricing adjustment for the year is large. A 20% penalty applies if the adjustment exceeds the lesser of $5 million or 10% of the company's gross receipts. A 40% penalty applies if it exceeds the lesser of $20 million or 20% of gross receipts.

Transactional penalty: applies when the price charged was far from the correct price. Roughly, a 20% penalty applies when the price was 200% or more (or 50% or less) of the correct arm's length price, and a 40% penalty applies at 400% or more (or 25% or less).

Important: the penalty is a percentage of the extra tax owed, not of the adjustment itself. Because the $20 million threshold is low for large multinationals, the 40% penalty can be a real risk.

How Companies Protect Themselves

The best defense is contemporaneous documentation. This is a written report, prepared by the time the tax return is filed, that explains the method the company used and why it was reasonable. If the tax authority asks for it, the company must provide it within 30 days. Good documentation can remove the penalty even if the price is later adjusted.

Tip: documentation written after an audit begins does not count as contemporaneous. The timing matters as much as the content.
